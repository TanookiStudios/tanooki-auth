What two-factor authentication is, in plain English

November 2, 2026

A password is one thing you know. If someone steals it, they can be you. Two-factor authentication (often shortened to 2FA, and also called multi-factor authentication or MFA) adds a second, different kind of proof before a website lets you in.

The three kinds of proof

The U.S. Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) describes three kinds: something you know (a password or PIN), something you have (your phone, with an app or a text message on it, or a security key) and something you are (your fingerprint or face). Two-factor means using two of those kinds, not two passwords.

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) puts it simply: with two-factor turned on, a crook who has your password still can’t sign in without the second piece.

Which second step to choose

The FTC lists the common options and how they stack up:

  • A code by text message or email. Much better than nothing.
  • A code from an authenticator app, like Tanooki Auth. The FTC calls this safer than text messages, because it doesn’t depend on your phone number.
  • A security key or passkey. The strongest option, because it can’t be typed into a fake website.

CISA’s advice is that any kind of MFA is better than none. Start with whatever your account offers, and upgrade later.

Where to turn it on first

CISA recommends turning it on for your email, social media, online shopping and banking accounts. If you only do one today, make it your email, because email is how most other passwords get reset. We explain why in Protect your email first.
