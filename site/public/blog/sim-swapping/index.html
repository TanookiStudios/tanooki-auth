SIM swapping: how scammers steal your phone number

November 2, 2026

Your phone number feels like it belongs to you. To your carrier, it’s an account that can be moved to a new SIM card, and a scammer can sometimes talk them into doing exactly that.

How a SIM swap works

The FTC explains it: a scammer contacts your carrier pretending to be you, says your phone was lost, and asks to move your number to a SIM card they control. Once it moves, your calls and texts, including your sign-in codes, go to them. Your own phone suddenly loses service.

It’s a real and growing problem

The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center reported in 2022 that it received 320 SIM swapping complaints from January 2018 to December 2020, with adjusted losses of about $12 million, and 1,611 complaints in 2021 alone, with adjusted losses of more than $68 million.

How to protect yourself

  • Move off text message codes. The FTC and FBI both recommend an authenticator app or a security key instead. A SIM swap can’t touch codes made on your device. See App codes vs text messages.
  • Add a PIN or passcode to your carrier account. The FTC suggests this.
  • Share less online. The FBI advises not posting your phone number, address or details about money or cryptocurrency you own.
  • Never give your carrier PIN to someone who calls you. Hang up and call your carrier’s real number.
  • Notice a sudden loss of service. If your phone shows “No service” for no reason, call your carrier from another phone right away.
