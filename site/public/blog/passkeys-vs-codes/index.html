Passkeys vs two-factor codes: what’s the difference?

November 2, 2026

More and more sites offer passkeys. You may wonder whether that makes your authenticator app pointless. It doesn’t, but passkeys are worth using where you can.

What a passkey is

A passkey lets you sign in with the same face, fingerprint or PIN you use to unlock your phone or computer. The FIDO Alliance, the industry group behind the standard, explains that there’s nothing to type, so there’s nothing to type into the wrong place.

Apple describes why that matters: the website only stores a public key, and the private key never leaves your devices. Google adds that passkeys can’t be written down, shared or accidentally given away, which makes them much harder to phish.

Why that beats a code

A six-digit code is something you can read and type, so a scammer can trick you into typing it into a fake page (see Phishing and fake login pages). A passkey is tied to the real website’s address and simply won’t work on a fake one. That’s why CISA ranks phishing-resistant methods like passkeys and security keys at the top.

So why keep an authenticator app?

  • Not every site offers passkeys yet. Many still only offer codes.
  • Some sites ask for a code as a backup or for certain actions.
  • Shared or borrowed computers. Google advises only creating passkeys on devices you personally own and use.

What to do

Use a passkey where a site offers one, starting with your email and your Apple or Google account. Keep an authenticator app for everything else, and keep recovery codes for both. Our Google guide covers turning on two-step verification, and Google’s help page above covers passkeys.
