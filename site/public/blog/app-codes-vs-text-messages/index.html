Authenticator app codes vs text message codes

November 2, 2026

Lots of websites offer to text you a six-digit code when you sign in. Many also let you use an authenticator app instead. Both give you a short code to type in, so what’s the difference?

Text messages travel through your phone number

A texted code is only as safe as your phone number. The FTC warns that in a SIM swap, a scammer talks your carrier into moving your number to their phone, and then your codes go to them. CISA’s fact sheet on phishing-resistant MFA goes further: it says text and voice codes are open to phishing, SIM swaps and attacks on the phone network itself, and should only be a last resort.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), which writes the federal government’s identity rules, labels codes sent over the phone network as “restricted”: allowed, but with extra conditions, because of these risks.

App codes never leave your device

An authenticator app makes the code on your phone or computer from a secret it saved when you scanned the setup QR code. Nothing is sent to you, so there’s nothing to intercept, and a SIM swap doesn’t touch it. It also works with no signal, on a plane or abroad.

Neither one stops a fake website

Be honest about the limit: CISA points out that if you type an app code into a convincing fake login page, the scammer can use it too. That’s why passkeys and security keys are rated higher (see Passkeys vs codes). Tanooki Auth’s browser extension helps here by filling a code only on the exact website an account belongs to.

What to do

  • Where a site offers an authenticator app, switch from text messages to the app.
  • Once the app works, remove your phone number as a sign-in option if the site lets you, so it can’t be used as a back door.
  • Save the site’s recovery codes (see Recovery codes).
